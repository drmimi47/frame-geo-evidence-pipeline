## 1. Deep history: the Great Meadow, the Kakhovka reservoir, Enerhodar, the thermal plant and the ZNPP

### Takeaway
The studio site has three layers. First, a Cossack floodplain: the Great Meadow held six of the eight Zaporozhian
Sich. Then a Soviet "sea", flooded in 1955–56 over up to 100 villages and about 37,000 people. Then an energy
landscape built on that water: Enerhodar was founded in 1970 for the thermal plant, the thermal plant ran from
1973–77, and the six reactors from 1985–1996. The 2023 breach drained the reservoir and uncovered the first layer again.

### Cited Findings
**The Great Meadow (Великий Луг) before the reservoir**
- A tangle of linked rivers and tributaries, reed lakes, swamps, meadows, floodplain forest, shrub and dunes. It ran more than 100 km east–west and 20–25 km north–south, between Babyne (Kherson oblast) and today's Nova Kakhovka — [Texty.org.ua, "Map of the Great Meadow", 23 Jan 2024, Tymoshenko, Kelm, Solodko, Hontsa](https://texty.org.ua/projects/111574/karta-velykoho-luhu-pyat-sichej-stavka-monholskoho-hana-ta-inshi-cikavi-miscya/) (fetched)
- In the 16th–18th centuries six of the eight Zaporozhian Sich stood on the Great Meadow — [Texty](https://texty.org.ua/projects/111574/karta-velykoho-luhu-pyat-sichej-stavka-monholskoho-hana-ta-inshi-cikavi-miscya/) (fetched)
- The Sich sites, per Texty (fetched):
  - Tomakivka Sich: late 16th century, on Tomakivka island.
  - Bazavluk Sich: 1593–1638, destroyed by Mykola Potocki's forces in summer 1638.
  - Chortomlyk (Stara) Sich: 1652–1709; Ivan Sirko lived there from 1663.
  - Mykytyn Sich: at Mykytyn Rih, below the Nikopol landing.
  - Kamianska Sich: 1709–11 and 1728–34.
  - Nova (Pidpilnenska) Sich: founded 1734 and destroyed on Catherine II's order in June 1775.
  — [Texty](https://texty.org.ua/projects/111574/karta-velykoho-luhu-pyat-sichej-stavka-monholskoho-hana-ta-inshi-cikavi-miscya/)
- Khortytsia, the best-known Sich island, is described as the upper end of the Great Meadow. One academic author argues the flooding of the Sich sites was, among practical aims, an attempt to erase Ukrainian memory, "not the main motive" — [Dub & Tereshkin, LDUFK repository (PDF)](https://repository.ldufk.edu.ua/bitstream/34606048/34850/1/Volodymyr%20Dub%2c.pdf) (snippet). Mykhailo Mulenko of the Khortytsia reserve also argues the sites were submerged deliberately — [Wikipedia, Kakhovka Reservoir](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kakhovka_Reservoir) (fetched)
- Other layers: the Golden Horde city of Orda / Shehr al-Jadid, Khan Mamai's seat in 1360–1374, about 30 km south of Zaporizhzhia; and the Kamianske hillfort at Kamianka-Dniprovska, said to be the centre of Great Scythia in the 6th–4th centuries BC, mostly submerged — [Texty](https://texty.org.ua/projects/111574/karta-velykoho-luhu-pyat-sichej-stavka-monholskoho-hana-ta-inshi-cikavi-miscya/) (fetched)
- Flooded villages named: Velyki Hyrla, Mali Hyrla, Zolota Balka, Hrushivskyi Kut and most of Novopavlivka; Pokrovske (on the Nova Sich site) was partly washed away. "Up to 100 villages" were fully or partly washed away, affecting about 37,000 people; more than 250,000 ha went under the "Kakhovka Sea" — [Texty](https://texty.org.ua/projects/111574/karta-velykoho-luhu-pyat-sichej-stavka-monholskoho-hana-ta-inshi-cikavi-miscya/) (fetched). The 37,000 figure is repeated in [Dub & Tereshkin](https://repository.ldufk.edu.ua/bitstream/34606048/34850/1/Volodymyr%20Dub%2c.pdf) (snippet)
- People were "presented with a fait accompli" and evicted from villages that later went under water — [fair.org.ua](https://www.fair.org.ua/en/kahovska-ges-pidryv-tragediya-yaka-zminyla-dnipro-nazavzhdy/) (snippet)

**The Kakhovka HPP and reservoir**
- Built 1950–1956 near Nova Kakhovka as one of the "great construction projects of communism"; it flooded more than 2,000 km² including the Great Meadow — [fair.org.ua](https://www.fair.org.ua/?p=21763) (snippet); [Meduza, 9 Jun 2023](https://meduza.io/feature/2023/06/09/kahovskaya-ges-byla-odnoy-iz-velikih-stroek-kommunizma-ona-polnostyu-razrushena) (snippet)
- The reservoir was filled in autumn 1955 and spring 1956 — [Meduza (via search summary)](https://meduza.io/feature/2023/06/09/kahovskaya-ges-byla-odnoy-iz-velikih-stroek-kommunizma-ona-polnostyu-razrushena) (snippet)
- Reservoir statistics: area 2,155 km², volume 18.2 km³, length 240 km, maximum width 23 km, depth 3–26 m (mean 8.4 m), surface 16 m; the dam raised the river by 16 m. Locals called it the "Kakhovka Sea" — [Wikipedia, Kakhovka Reservoir](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kakhovka_Reservoir) (fetched)
- What the water served: irrigation (the Kakhovka and Krasnoznamianka systems), the North Crimean Canal, the Dnipro–Kryvyi Rih canal, navigation, fish farms, and cooling for the ZNPP — [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kakhovka_Reservoir) (fetched)
- The HPP was rated at 357 MW. Fred Pearce notes the reservoir flooded 830 square miles, more than three times Hoover Dam's land take, for less than a fifth of its power — [Yale e360, 21 Oct 2024](https://e360.yale.edu/features/ukraine-war-wilding) (fetched)

**Enerhodar and the Zaporizhzhia Thermal Power Plant (ZTPP)**
- Enerhodar was founded on 12 June 1970 to build and serve the Zaporizhzhia thermal power station, and became a city in 1985. Population: 56,180 (2001 census); 52,237 (2022 estimate). 2001 census: 57.14% Ukrainian and 39.83% Russian, about 62% mainly Russian-speaking — [Wikipedia, Enerhodar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Enerhodar) (fetched)
- ZTPP: a first stage of 1,200 MW from 1973 and a second of 2,400 MW from 1977. It was one of Europe's largest thermal plants at 3,600 MW — [Wikipedia, ZTPS](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zaporizhzhia_thermal_power_station) (snippet); [ESU (PDF)](https://www.esu.com.ua/pdf/file/15427.pdf) (snippet)
- Current capacity figures conflict: 2,850 MW (2×300 + 2×325 + 2×800) per [Wikipedia's list](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_power_stations_in_Ukraine) (snippet), against 1,250 MW in four coal units per [GEM wiki](https://www.gem.wiki/Zaporizhia_power_station) (snippet). The operator is DTEK (Dniproenergo). It stopped in May 2022 when its coal ran out under occupation — [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zaporizhzhia_thermal_power_station) (snippet)
- The ZTPP's 330 kV switchyard is where Ferosplavna-1, the plant's backup line, leaves — see `forensic_precedents_analogues.md`, citing [Ukranews](https://ukranews.com/en/news/880261-last-power-line-that-connected-zaporizhzhia-npp-with-ukraine-s-power-system-turned-off-energoatom)

**The ZNPP**
- Six VVER-1000/320 pressurised-water reactors, about 950 MWe each (5,700 MWe; 6 × 3,000 MWth). In 2020 it made nearly half of Ukraine's nuclear electricity and more than a fifth of all its electricity. Dry cask storage on site since 2004 — [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zaporizhzhia_Nuclear_Power_Plant) (fetched)
- Construction start and commissioning, per the infobox:

  | Unit | Construction | Commissioned |
  |---|---|---|
  | 1 | 1 Apr 1980 | 25 Dec 1985 |
  | 2 | 1 Jan 1981 | 15 Feb 1986 |
  | 3 | 1 Apr 1982 | 5 Mar 1987 |
  | 4 | 1 Apr 1983 | 14 Apr 1988 |
  | 5 | 1 Nov 1985 | 27 Oct 1989 |
  | 6 | 1 Jun 1986 | 17 Sep 1996 (lead text says 1995) |

  — [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zaporizhzhia_Nuclear_Power_Plant) (fetched)
- Europe's largest nuclear plant by capacity; units entered commercial operation 1985–1996 — [WNN, Feb 2026](https://world-nuclear-news.org/articles/russian-regulator-issues-operating-licence-for-second-zaporizhzhia-unit) (fetched)
- About 11,500 staff before the war (IAEA figure, as reported) — [Nikvesti](https://nikvesti.com/en/news/public/licensed-specialists-left-znpp-staff-reduced-by-8000) (snippet); about 11,000 Enerhodar residents worked at the plant — [Wikipedia, Enerhodar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Enerhodar) (fetched)

**Licences and life extension under Ukraine (relevant to the later Russian licences)**
- SNRIU licensed Unit 1 beyond its 30-year design life until **23 December 2025** — [NS Energy](https://www.nsenergybusiness.com/news/newsukraines-zaporozhye-unit-1-to-restart-after-life-extension-work-5016731/) (snippet)
- Unit 5 was cleared to operate until 2030 — [WNN](https://www.world-nuclear-news.org/Articles/Ukraines-Zaporozhe-5-cleared-to-operate-until-2030) (snippet)
- Unit 4's licence was extended for a decade — [UNIAN](https://www.unian.info/economics/10296753-operating-license-of-zaporizhia-npp-s-reactor-4-extended-for-another-decade.html) (snippet)

**Westinghouse fuel (the plant's "Western" turn)**
- Westinghouse fuel came to Ukraine first at South Ukraine 3 in 2005 (six TVS-W pilot assemblies). After damage in 2012 it was suspended pending redesign — [WNN](https://wnn.world-nuclear.org/articles/south-ukraine-3-fully-loaded-with-westinghouse-fue) (snippet)
- ZNPP Unit 5 became the second Ukrainian unit to run fully on Westinghouse (TVS-WR) fuel, 163 assemblies, announced 12 December 2019 — [NucNet](https://www.nucnet.org/news/zaporozhye-5-becomes-second-unit-to-use-only-westinghouse-fuel-12-3-2019) (snippet). Units 1, 3 and 4 had mixed cores (126 TVS-WR assemblies combined, March 2021) — [NEI Magazine](https://www.neimagazine.com/news/zaporozhye-5-fully-loaded-with-westinghouse-fuel-7561138/) (snippet)
- On 20 April 2023 Rosenergoatom announced it would replace the US-made fuel with Russian fuel, after a US Department of Energy letter on export-controlled data — [WNA](https://world-nuclear.org/information-library/country-profiles/countries-t-z/ukraine-russia-war-and-nuclear-energy) (fetched)

### Inferences
- The drained bed is a palimpsest: Sich sites, villages and kurhans drowned for the reservoir, uncovered by the reservoir's destruction. For the film, the 1950s flooding (people moved, history drowned for power) and the 2023 draining (power lost, history uncovered) can be framed as two violent edits of one landscape. The project's "Great Meadow outline must hold for earlier states of the river" rule already fits this.
- Enerhodar ("energy-gift") is a single-purpose town. It was founded for the thermal plant and grew with the nuclear plant, and in 2001 it had a large Russian-speaking population. That makes it a clear case of a Soviet company town, and partly explains the occupation's politics of staff and passports.
- Westinghouse fuel at Unit 5 and in the mixed cores is a material sign of Ukraine's turn West before the war. The 2023 switch back to Russian fuel is a material sign of the occupation.
- Russia's Unit 1 licence (December 2025) arrived in the month Ukraine's own life-extension licence for Unit 1 expired (23 December 2025). This looks like a deliberate legal handover, but no source says so.

### Gaps
- No authoritative English source fetched on the Kakhovka HPP's construction workforce, the exact number of villages flooded, or where their people were resettled. Texty's "up to 100 villages, ~37,000 people" is the best figure found. Local archives (Nikopol, Kamianka-Dniprovska museums) and Ukrainian historiography (e.g., Dmytro Yavornytsky's pre-flood descriptions of the Great Meadow) are the next step.
- Not found: who planned Enerhodar (the architects, master plan) or its first population.
- Not found: the ZTPP's state after 2022 beyond "out of coal". Its switchyard's condition matters for Ferosplavna-1.
- Not confirmed: the Westinghouse fuel status of each unit at the time of seizure in 2022.

## 2. The occupation timeline, 2022–2026

### Takeaway
The plant was taken by force on the night of 3–4 March 2022 and turned into a Russian state asset by decree on
5 October 2022. Since 1 September 2022 the IAEA has kept a permanent mission on site. All six reactors have been
shut down since 2022, cooled through ever fewer lines, and the plant has lost all off-site power about 26 times
(WNA count to 20 August 2026). Every reconnection since October 2025 has needed an IAEA-brokered local ceasefire.

## 3. Law: Article 56, occupation, the IAEA and UN resolutions, the ICC, licences and claims

### Takeaway
The legal frame has four layers:
- Humanitarian law: AP I Article 56 protects nuclear generating stations, and both Russia and Ukraine are parties to AP I. Russia's military use of the site arguably breaches Articles 56(5) and 58, and possibly the ICC crime of using human shields.
- Institutional condemnation: four IAEA Board resolutions, a General Conference resolution, and UNGA resolution A/RES/78/316 of 11 July 2024.
- Russia's domestic "legalisation": the 5 October 2022 decree and Rostekhnadzor licences, which Ukraine and most states treat as null.
- Civil claims: Energoatom's notice of an investment-treaty claim for more than $3 billion.

No court has yet ruled on the plant itself. The ICC warrants on Ukraine's energy grid are the nearest precedent.

### Cited Findings
**Additional Protocol I, Article 56 ("works and installations containing dangerous forces")**
- Article 56(1) protects dams, dykes and nuclear electrical generating stations even when they are military objectives, if an attack "may cause the release of dangerous forces and consequent severe losses among the civilian population". Tom Dannenbaum reads it as an ex ante prohibition: the risk alone is enough — [Lieber Institute, Dannenbaum, 13 Mar 2022](https://lieber.westpoint.edu/attack-zaporizhzhia-nuclear-plant/) (fetched)
- Article 56(2)(b): protection ceases for a nuclear station only if it supplies power in "regular, significant and direct support of military operations" and attack is "the only feasible way to terminate such support". Feeding a general grid may not qualify, and striking transmission lines at a distance is a feasible alternative — [Dannenbaum, 13 Mar 2022](https://lieber.westpoint.edu/attack-zaporizhzhia-nuclear-plant/) (fetched)
- Both Russia and Ukraine are parties to AP I, so it applies to their international armed conflict — [Dannenbaum](https://lieber.westpoint.edu/attack-zaporizhzhia-nuclear-plant/) (fetched). Russia's 2019 step revoked only its Article 90 declaration accepting the Fact-Finding Commission, and its AP I obligations remain in force — [Swiss notification](https://www.dfae.admin.ch/dam/eda/fr/documents/aussenpolitik/voelkerrecht/genevenotifications/191030-GENEVE_e.pdf) (snippet); [zn.ua](https://zn.ua/ukr/international/gumanitarniy-demarsh-331417_.html) (snippet)
- The treaty rule is stricter than the customary rule (ICRC Rule 42) — [Dannenbaum](https://lieber.westpoint.edu/attack-zaporizhzhia-nuclear-plant/) (fetched). The US is not a party and objects to AP I's Article 56 provisions — [search summary of Lieber/DoD commentary](https://lieber.westpoint.edu/?p=13615) (snippet)
- On the 4 March 2022 attack: if the shelling risked a radioactive release, "it is almost certain that this operation violated" Article 56; the grave breach (Art. 85(3)(c)) is less likely to be provable because it needs knowledge of excessive harm — [Dannenbaum](https://lieber.westpoint.edu/attack-zaporizhzhia-nuclear-plant/) (fetched)
- On military use of the plant:
  - Article 56(5) says parties shall "endeavour to avoid locating any military objectives in the vicinity". The exception for purely defensive installations likely does not apply, because the site was reportedly used to strike nearby towns.
  - "The deliberate exploitation of co-location entails an endeavor to co-locate, rather than an endeavor to avoid it", which breaches Articles 56(5) and 58.
  - Forces involved could be liable for the ICC war crime of using human shields (Art. 8(2)(b)(xxiii)).
  - Ukraine is barred from reprisals against the plant (Art. 56(4)).
  - A demilitarised zone needs both parties' agreement (Art. 60); Article 56(6) only "urges" such agreements.
  — [Dannenbaum, Lieber Institute, 22 Aug 2022](https://lieber.westpoint.edu/escalating-military-use-zaporizhzhia-nuclear-plant/) (fetched)
- More legal commentary: [EJIL:Talk!, on Chernobyl and Zaporizhzhia](https://www.ejiltalk.org/legal-implications-of-the-military-operations-at-the-chernobyl-and-zaporizhzhya-nuclear-power-plants/) (not read); [Völkerrechtsblog](https://voelkerrechtsblog.org/de/how-are-nuclear-power-plants-protected-by-law-during-war/) (not read)

## 4. Politics and futures: peace talks, restart, decommissioning, and the reservoir's future

### Takeaway
The plant is a bargaining chip in US-brokered talks, and no deal had been reached by late October 2026:
- US proposals: Trump's March 2025 idea of US "ownership"; Witkoff's framework of US control with power shared; the December 2025 draft's three-way operation with the US as primary overseer.
- Ukraine: a US–Ukraine joint venture, 50/50, plus demilitarisation of the plant, Enerhodar and the Kakhovka HPP.
- Russia: Russian control, with cheap power perhaps sold to Ukraine.

A restart needs water, lines and staff that do not now exist. The reservoir's future (rebuild vs. a "new Great Meadow")
is written into the same peace draft (point 12).

### Inferences
- Futures to name in the film. These are scenarios, not forecasts; each depends on the war's outcome.
  1. Frozen occupation: cold shutdown and a single-line life, with Rostekhnadzor licences accumulating.
  2. A Russian restart for occupied territories: needs a reconnection south, water (the floating pump station), and staff.
  3. A brokered arrangement (US-operated or shared), the shape of point 12.
  4. Return to Ukraine and inspection, perhaps leading to decommissioning of units whose licences have lapsed. Unit 1's Ukrainian licence ran to December 2025.
  5. Accident: the scenario every IAEA update warns of.
- Point 12 joins the plant to "the restoration of the Kakhovka HPP". A peace deal could therefore decide the studio site: if the dam is rebuilt, the Great Meadow goes back under water. The Great Meadow is in the peace plan, not only in the war.
- The US crypto-mining and data-centre talk turns the plant into a pure electricity commodity, cut off from the grid that made it. That opposes the film's view of the plant as a node in a living network.

## 7. Further lines of inquiry for the film

### Takeaway
The strongest openings beyond the current work:
- the IAEA's updates as a dataset and the IAEA as a witness;
- the Great Meadow as a peace-plan object (point 12);
- the legal handover of Unit 1 (Ukrainian licence to December 2025, Russian licence from December 2025);
- the staff's passports and contracts;
- the sound of the lines, contrasted with the Hum;
- comparison with Chernobyl in 2022 and Kursk in 2024.

### Cited Findings
- **The IAEA as witness, its updates as data.** The IAEA has had staff on site since 1 September 2022 and numbers its updates (at least 364 by October 2026). Its counts (losses of power, ceasefires, wells, pond levels) are a time series — [WNA](https://world-nuclear.org/information-library/country-profiles/countries-t-z/ukraine-russia-war-and-nuclear-energy) (fetched); [IAEA Update 364 URL](https://www.iaea.org/newscenter/pressreleases/update-364-iaea-director-general-statement-on-situation-in-ukraine) (exists, not readable: HTTP 402). Two official accounts of one plant, both filed with one agency, make a natural split-screen.
- **The protection zone that never was.** Proposed 6 Sep 2022, given up on 30 Mar 2023, never given a size — [WNN](https://www.world-nuclear-news.org/Articles/IAEA-calls-for-nuclear-safety-exclusion-zone-aroun); [SCR](https://www.securitycouncilreport.org/whatsinblue/2023/05/ukraine-briefing-on-the-zaporizhzhia-nuclear-power-plant-2.php) (project config).
